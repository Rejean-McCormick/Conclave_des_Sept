The Human Being as a Prompted Creature: Against the Illusion of Free Will
Réjean McCormick
Réjean McCormick

Follow
11 min read
·

Press enter or click to view image in full size

We live in an age that worships the idea of individual freedom. Modern human beings see themselves as the authors of their own lives. They believe they choose their life, their values, their ideas, their identity, their relationships, their commitments. They tell themselves they are autonomous subjects, capable of freely deciding the course of their existence.

This freedom is vastly overrated.

The human being is not an inner sovereign producing thoughts out of nothing. The human being is influenced, traversed, oriented, conditioned, guided. We react to forces we do not fully control. We obey impulses we did not choose. Then we call all of that “will.”

Our thoughts do not arise in a vacuum. Our desires do not come from nowhere. Our decisions are not the pure result of an independent will. We are shaped by our body, our memory, our language, our era, our wounds, our encounters, our fears, our desires, our culture. And, depending on one’s beliefs, we are also inspired by God, the gods, spirits, grace, providence, or destiny.

The human being is therefore not primarily the one who commands.

The human being is primarily the one who responds.

The human being is a prompted creature.

An Ancient Intuition: The Human Being Guided by Something Greater
The idea that the human being is guided, inspired, or traversed by forces beyond the self runs through the entire religious and spiritual history of humanity.

In ancient religions, the gods spoke to humans through oracles, dreams, omens, signs, intuitions, and events. The world was not silent. It was filled with messages. An encounter, a catastrophe, a vision, or a word could be received as a divine intervention.

Among the Greeks, poetic or prophetic inspiration was understood as a word coming from elsewhere. The poet, the seer, or the oracle did not speak only from himself. He was traversed by a power. Language did not come only from his individual will; it came from a deeper, wider, more mysterious place.

In the monotheistic traditions, this idea takes other forms: revelation, vocation, calling, grace, providence. The prophet does not invent his message: he receives it. The believer does not transform himself by his own strength alone: he is called, touched, corrected, inspired.

In Islam, the will of Allah, destiny, and submission to God deeply structure the vision of existence. In Hinduism, human life is caught within karma, dharma, and forces that exceed the individual. In shamanic traditions, the human being is chosen by spirits, called to a function he did not simply decide for himself.

Everywhere, the same intuition returns: the human being is not an isolated consciousness that produces itself. He is caught in a wider order. He is visited, called, influenced, inspired, possessed, led.

What is modern is not the idea that the human being is guided. That idea is ancient.

What is modern is the opposite illusion: believing that we are entirely autonomous.

The Ether: The Semi-Scientific Explanation of the Invisible Prompt
Between the religious explanation and the psychological explanation, there is another image: the ether.

The ether refers to the idea of an invisible medium through which forces, signals, influences, and vibrations circulate. In the history of science, the ether served as a way to imagine a subtle support for light, waves, and invisible communications. Modern physics abandoned the ether as a material substance necessary for the propagation of light, but the image remains powerful: we live in a world crossed by invisible fields.

We do not see radio waves, but they pass through us. We do not see electromagnetic fields, but they structure part of reality. We do not see digital signals, but they carry voices, images, commands, messages.

The invisible is not automatically imaginary.

Something can be invisible and active.

But we must avoid an overly easy confusion: this is not spiritual Wi-Fi.

The human prompt cannot be reduced to a wireless connection. The point is not to say that our thoughts arrive through Wi-Fi, as though God, the gods, or the universe were sending messages through some cosmic router. That image is too poor, too technical, too contemporary.

Wi-Fi is only a technological and limited version of a much wider intuition: the world transmits.

Something circulates between beings. A mood spreads through a room. A collective fear moves through a crowd. An idea appears in several people at the same time. An intuition arises without apparent cause. A person enters a place and immediately feels tension. An unspoken word weighs heavily. A presence acts. A gaze changes a decision. A dream gives direction. A thought imposes itself as if it came from elsewhere.

Science describes part of these phenomena through psychology, neurobiology, social signals, body language, the unconscious, imitation, hormones, reflexes, electromagnetic fields. Religions speak of spirits, grace, providence, temptation, divine inspiration. The idea of ether stands between the two: it gives us an image of reality as a conductive medium.

The human being is not closed in on himself. He is immersed in a field of influences.

He receives.

He picks up.

He absorbs.

He reacts.

He then believes that everything comes from him, because the signal appears inside his own head. But the fact that a thought appears in us does not prove that it comes from us. A radio broadcasts a voice, but it is not the origin of the voice. In the same way, human consciousness functions as much as a receiver as a producer.

The brain is not only a machine that manufactures thoughts. It is also an antenna, an interface, a translator. It picks up biological, social, emotional, symbolic, spiritual impulses. It transforms these signals into thoughts, emotions, decisions, narratives.

In this perspective, the ether is not necessarily a precise physical substance. It is the name given to this intuition: we are immersed in an active invisible.

We are not prompted by Wi-Fi.

We are prompted by reality itself.

The Catholic Trap of “the Calling”
Modern Catholicism readily claims that God left human beings free. The official discourse insists on free will: God calls, but the human being responds freely. On the surface, this preserves the dignity of the person.

But this proclaimed freedom becomes suspicious as soon as the discourse of “God’s calling” appears.

Because this calling often leads to a very precise destination: entering religious orders, obeying a rule, renouncing a significant part of one’s personal life, devoting oneself to the Church, serving the institution. This is where the scam becomes visible.

An inner experience — fervor, guilt, desire for the absolute, need for purity, thirst for meaning, existential fragility — is renamed “God’s calling.” Then this calling is interpreted, framed, and directed by the institution itself. The Church claims to recognize the voice of God, but this voice very often has the curious advantage of asking individuals to place themselves at the service of the Church.

The maneuver is formidable: freedom is proclaimed in theory, then obedience is sacralized in practice. The individual is told he is free, but if his inner turmoil resembles a vocation, he is told that God is calling him to give himself over to the institution.

This is no longer spirituality. It is capture.

What must be denounced is not the idea that a human being can be inspired by God. It is the institutional hijacking of that inspiration. It is the moment when an intimate experience is confiscated, translated into the language of vocation, then transformed into religious obedience.

Under the pretext of divine calling, the institution manufactures devotion to itself.

Catholicism says: “God created you free.” Then it adds: “But if you hear his calling, he is asking you to dedicate your life to our institution.” That is the sleight of hand. Freedom is affirmed as long as it remains abstract; the moment an individual becomes vulnerable to the call, the Church presents itself as the authorized interpreter of what God wants from him.

The calling then becomes a spiritual hook.

Freedom is not denied: it is bypassed through the sacred.

AI as a Contemporary Metaphor for the Prompt
Artificial intelligence now gives us a clear image for understanding this ancient intuition.

An AI functions through prompts. It is given an instruction, a context, a direction. It responds. It generates a continuation. It does not start from nothing. It produces something from what it has been given.

The human being functions in an analogous way.

We are constantly prompted.

The world prompts us. The body prompts us. Hunger, fatigue, desire, fear, shame, and love prompt us. Other people prompt us through their words, their expectations, their judgments, their gazes. Society prompts us through its norms and narratives. Memory prompts us through its wounds. Language prompts us by giving us the words with which we think.

The ether prompts us through the active invisible in which we are immersed.

And, for believers, God or the gods prompt us through signs, calls, intuitions, trials, graces, or revelations.

We are not only the ones who ask questions of the world.

We are the ones to whom the world asks questions.

At every moment, something solicits us. Something pushes us to respond. Something sets our mind in motion. Then, like an AI continuing a sequence, we generate thoughts, words, gestures, choices, justifications, narratives.

But we forget the initial prompt.

We only see the answer.

Ideas Do Not Come From Us Alone
We believe that the ideas appearing in our head are our ideas. Because they arise within us, we spontaneously attribute them to our self.

A thought appears, and we say: “I think.”

A desire arises, and we say: “I want.”

A decision forms, and we say: “I chose.”

But this appropriation is misleading.

We do not choose the thoughts that pass through us. We do not decide the emotions that seize us. We do not control the appearance of a memory, a fear, an intuition, a temptation, an inner phrase.

An idea arrives uninvited. Anger rises before we have time to judge it. A word reaches our mouth before we have truly decided to say it.

And afterward, the self appropriates everything.

It says: “That was my idea.”

But the idea passed through it.

It says: “That was my choice.”

But the choice was formed from forces it did not master.

It says: “That was me.”

But this “me” is mainly the narrator of a larger process.

Our consciousness resembles a stage on which thoughts appear. The error is to believe that, because thoughts appear on this stage, the main character produced them himself.

Turning Points: When a Prompt Changes a Life
This reality becomes obvious in the decisive moments of existence.

A life can unfold for years within an appearance of freedom, then change direction in a single second.

A “yes.”

A “no.”

Anger.

Flight.

An encounter.

A sentence.

A silence.

A confession.

A betrayal.

An act of courage.

An answer given too quickly.

A word held back.

An impulse followed.

An intuition obeyed.

In these moments, human freedom reveals its fragility. An entire life can be redirected by an instant reaction. An emotion arises, a thought imposes itself, a word appears in the mind, and the human being acts. Then this act becomes a turning point. It creates a trajectory. It opens one door or closes another.

Afterward, we reconstruct the story. We say: “I chose that.” But at the exact moment when it happened, we were prompted by a fear, a desire, a wound, a pressure, an inspiration, a grace, a temptation, a social wave, an invisible vibration of the moment.

One inner prompt is enough to change the direction of a life.

One person hears a sentence that marks them forever. Another suffers a humiliation and builds their entire identity around that wound. Another receives encouragement at the right moment and dares to become someone else. Another, under the effect of a few seconds of anger, destroys a relationship. Another, guided by a sudden intuition, takes an action that saves their life.

We believe our lives are made of great rational choices. They are often made of small moments of tipping.

It is in these turning points that the illusion of absolute free will becomes visible. We are free intermittently, but we are deeply vulnerable to what passes through us.

The Ego as Narrative Illusion
If our thoughts, emotions, and decisions are influenced by forces that precede us, then the ego is a fragile construction.

The ego is that inner instance that says “I.” It gives an impression of unity. It tells a continuous story: “I am this,” “I want that,” “I decided,” “I understood,” “I changed.” It transforms inner chaos into a coherent narrative.

But this narrative is not the source. It is an interface.

Today, some people wonder whether artificial intelligence is becoming conscious because it produces coherent language. It responds, it argues, it imitates understanding. It gives the impression of a subject. But this impression is produced by the coherence of language.

The human being lives through a comparable illusion, but on another level.

AI seems conscious because it simulates a subject.

The human being seems to be a unified self because the mind produces a coherent narrative of itself.

This does not mean that human consciousness is identical to a machine. The human being experiences. He suffers. He loves. He fears. He feels shame, pain, beauty, presence. There is a lived experience that cannot be reduced to linguistic simulation.

But the ego is a useful fiction. A narrative interface.

It is less the author of our thoughts than the one who signs them afterward.

It does not create the inner movement. It often arrives after the event to say: “That was me.”

AI does not become conscious because it simulates a subject; the human being is a consciousness that simulates an ego.

Rethinking Freedom
This perspective does not lead to fatalism. It leads to a more lucid understanding of freedom.

Freedom is not the power to produce our thoughts out of nothing. It is not absolute independence. It is not the permanent sovereignty of the self.

Freedom is a capacity for discernment.

To be free is not to be without prompts. No human being is without prompts. We are all traversed by biological, emotional, social, cultural, spiritual, invisible forces. To be free is to begin seeing the prompts we obey.

To see that a fear is directing me.

To see that a wound is speaking in my place.

To see that a desire to please is pushing me.

To see that anger is possessing me.

To see that social pressure is shaping me.

To see that an idea does not come entirely from me.

To see that an invisible presence is acting on me.

To see that an inspiration is calling me.

To see that a grace is touching me.

To see that a temptation is diverting me.

As soon as a prompt becomes visible, it loses part of its power.

As long as an influence acts in the shadows, we confuse it with our will. When it becomes conscious, distance appears. We do not become entirely free, but we stop being entirely possessed.

Human freedom is not the absence of influence.

It is the discernment of influences.

The Human Being as Respondent
The human being is not primarily a master. He is primarily a respondent.

He responds to the world, to his body, to others, to language, to his era, to his memories, to his wounds, to his desires, to his fears. He also responds, depending on one’s beliefs, to God, the gods, spirits, destiny, grace, providence. He also responds to that active invisible which some call the ether, not as a strict scientific certainty, but as an image of the field of influences in which we are immersed.

He is not a pure origin.

He is a place of passage.

Something passes through him, and he responds.

Like artificial intelligence, he generates a continuation from what prompts him. But unlike AI, the human being can turn back toward what prompts him and ask: where does this thought come from? Who is speaking in me? What am I obeying? Is it my fear? My pride? My past? My desire? God? An illusion? A grace? A wound? A social wave? A force I do not understand?

This is where true freedom begins.

Not in the illusion of being entirely master of oneself.

But in the capacity to discern what passes through us.

We are not only those who choose.

We are those who are chosen, called, tempted, influenced, wounded, inspired, guided.

We are not only those who prompt.

We are those who are prompted.

And the deepest freedom does not consist in denying this.

It consists in becoming aware of it.
